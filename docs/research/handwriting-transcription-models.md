# Pick Gemini for Accuracy, GLM-OCR for Speed

For the most accurate handwriting transcription in late 2026, use **Google Gemini 3.1 Pro**. It ranks first of 18 models on WildHandBench, the newest independent real-world handwriting benchmark, and on free text it even beats a human single-pass baseline ([WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959v1)). For a fast, open-source model that runs locally on a Mac, use **GLM-OCR**. It is a 0.9-billion-parameter, MIT-licensed model that reads 96.8% of characters correctly on IAM handwriting lines ([withinnoise.dev](https://withinnoise.dev/blog/posts/ocr-benchmark/)). On its maker's handwriting test it scores within 3 points of Gemini 3 Pro, and it has the highest throughput in that test ([GLM-OCR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10910v2)). It also runs through Ollama and llama.cpp. The best practical system for live webcam transcription uses both. Cut each new line out of the camera frame, read it locally with GLM-OCR, and (optionally) send finished lines to a cloud Gemini model as a second opinion. Show the places where the two disagree; do not let either model "fix" the text. Three facts shape this advice. First, the top open OCR models are tied within about 2 points on clean handwriting, and photographed pages lose about 20 points compared with clean scans ([Docsumo](https://www.docsumo.com/blog/best-ocr-models)). So your camera setup matters more than which top model you choose. Second, most model errors are "autocorrect" errors: 63–91% of them come from language guessing, not from bad vision ([WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22959)). Third, the newest models (Claude 5.5, GPT-5.5/5.6, Gemini 3.6/3.8 Flash) have no independent handwriting results yet, and no benchmark tests handwriting from live video. That makes a 50-line test set of your own handwriting, captured with your own camera, the benchmark that decides. Grown to 100–300 lines, the same data is enough to fine-tune a model and cut its errors by roughly a quarter to a half.

## Five picks cover accuracy, local speed, latency and personalization

The table below gives the direct answers. "CER" means **character error rate**: the share of characters the model gets wrong, so lower is better. Some benchmarks report "accuracy" instead, which is roughly 100 minus CER.

| Goal | Top pick | Main evidence | Runner-up |
|---|---|---|---|

| Highest accuracy, open, still local-sized | **dots.mocr** (3B, MIT) | 98.0% on IAM lines, top of 22 open engines | Qwen3.5 9B / Qwen3-VL-8B (general models, 97.3% / strong zero-shot) |
| Lowest latency (tens of milliseconds) | **CTC line recognizer** (PyLaia-class, HTR-ConvText) | about 10 ms per line; 4.0–4.7% CER on IAM | TrOCR (2.9–4.2% CER, slower) |
| Fine-tuning on your own handwriting | **LightOnOCR 1B** or **GLM-OCR** | A 1B LightOnOCR fine-tune cut CER from 8.97% to 2.06%; GLM-OCR has an official LLaMA-Factory recipe | Qwen3-VL-8B with LoRA (Unsloth) |

The rest of this report explains why these picks win, where the evidence is weak, and how to build the system.

## Gemini 3.1 Pro leads every 2026 handwriting test that includes it

Three independent 2026 studies tested current closed models on handwriting, and Gemini Pro came first in all three. **WildHandBench** (August 2026) tested 18 models on 500 real handwritten documents in four languages and nine real-life scenarios ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22959)). A study of **49 real handwritten maternity forms** from South Africa compared 9 frontier models ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16504v1)). **AIMultiple's cursive test** used 100 cursive paragraphs from 10 writers who were not asked to write neatly ([AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/handwriting-recognition)).

| Model | WildHandBench overall (higher is better) | WildHandBench free-text edit distance (lower is better) | Medical forms CER, real forms | Medical forms hallucination rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | **71.85** | **24.32** | **0.31** | 8% |
| Gemini 3.5 Flash | 68.25 | 27.10 | – | – |
| Claude Opus 4.8 | 57.70 | 53.30 | – | – |
| Claude Sonnet 4.6 | – | – | 0.37 | 12% |
| GPT-5.4 | – | – | 0.43 | **6%** |
| Human single pass | 77.09 | 30.08 | – | – |

Sources: [WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959v1); [medical forms](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16504v1).

On free text, **both Gemini models beat the human baseline** (24.32 and 27.10 vs 30.08). The authors warn that their human annotators were native Chinese speakers, which may make the human English score look worse than it should ([WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959v1)). AIMultiple's cursive test gives the same order: Gemini 3 Pro 1.00, GPT-5 0.97, Claude Sonnet 4.5 0.91. Its score is a coarse "semantic similarity", not CER, so it can hide small character errors ([AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/handwriting-recognition)). The GLM-OCR team's own handwriting test also puts Gemini 3 Pro on top (90.0), ahead of GPT-5.2 (78.0) ([GLM-OCR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10910v2)). A historian who transcribed about 10,000 words of 18th–19th-century English records chose Gemini 3 Pro as the main transcriber and reached **1.67% CER** ([Generative History](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/when-models-disagree-transcription)).

**Claude results depend on the model.** Claude Sonnet 4.6 did well on forms: it had the lowest CER on the synthetic forms (0.14 vs Gemini's 0.15) and the best results on formatted fields such as dates and numbers ([medical forms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16504)). But Claude Opus 4.8 was far behind on free-text handwriting (53.30 vs 24.32) ([WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959v1)). The only 2026 result for a Claude 5 model is on Urdu handwriting, where Claude Sonnet 5 had 0.266 CER against 0.172 for the cheaper Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite ([Urdu study](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.01635)). **GPT-5.4 invents the least text** (6% hallucination rate) but has a higher CER than Gemini 3.1 Pro (0.43 vs 0.31) ([medical forms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16504)).

**Classic OCR APIs are faster but less accurate on cursive.** In AIMultiple's test, Azure (0.90), Google Vision (0.87), Mistral OCR (0.85) and Amazon Textract (0.82) all trail the frontier LLMs ([AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/handwriting-recognition)). Mistral's own test claims Mistral OCR 3 beats Azure on handwriting (88.9 vs 78.2), which conflicts with AIMultiple ([PyImageSearch](https://pyimagesearch.com/2025/12/23/mistral-ocr-3-technical-review-sota-document-parsing-at-commodity-pricing/)). Their main advantage is speed: Azure AI Vision's synchronous OCR answers in about **300–400 ms**, while Azure Document Intelligence Read takes 1–2 s ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5923493/ocr-latency-azure-ai-vision-imageanalysis-analyze)).

**The biggest gap is that the newest models are not tested.** No independent handwriting benchmark yet covers Claude Opus/Sonnet/Haiku 5.5, GPT-5.5/5.6 or Gemini 3.6/3.8 Flash. The "Gemini 3.5 Flash" tested in WildHandBench is not on Google's current price list; the current Flash models are 3.6 and 3.8 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash are retired on December 1, 2026 ([Gemini API pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). Newer Flash models are probably at least as good, but no one has measured it. Prices are low enough for live use: Gemini 3.8 Flash costs $0.75 per million input tokens and 3.1 Pro costs $2.00 ([Gemini API pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)), so one webcam frame costs a fraction of a cent. Turn off or limit "thinking" for live use, because thinking tokens add cost and delay.

## Small open OCR models read clean handwriting lines at 96–98% accuracy

The only independent head-to-head of open OCR models on handwriting is a single-author benchmark of 22 engines on **60 IAM English handwriting lines** ([withinnoise.dev](https://withinnoise.dev/blog/posts/ocr-benchmark/)). It is small and not peer-reviewed, and IAM is public, so some models may have seen it in training. Still, the pattern is clear: the top six models are within about 2 points, and a few popular models fail badly.

| Model | Size | License | IAM lines accuracy | Other handwriting evidence | Mac / local path |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| dots.mocr | 3B | MIT | **98.0** | – | llama.cpp PR, community GGUF |
| Chandra OCR 2 | 5.3B | Restricted OpenRAIL-M (revenue limit) | 97.7 | No benchmark published | vLLM / HF only |
| olmOCR 2 | 8.3B | Apache 2.0 (research use note) | 97.4 | AIMultiple cursive 0.96 | Needs NVIDIA GPU ≥12 GB |
| Qwen3.5 9B (general VLM) | 9B | – | 97.3 | – | – |
| Qwen3.5 2B (general VLM) | 2B | – | 97.1 | – | – |
| **GLM-OCR** | 0.9B | MIT | **96.8** | Vendor test 87.0 (Gemini 3 Pro 90.0) | **Ollama, official GGUF** |
| LightOnOCR-2 | 1B | Apache 2.0 | 96.3 | IAM page CER 3.71% (1B LightOnOCR, ExpertHTR) | Transformers, CPU |
| PaddleOCR-VL 1.6 | 0.9B | Apache 2.0 | 95.8 | Vendor test 87.4 (v1.5) | MLX 4-bit, GGUF, Docker |
| DeepSeek-OCR 2 | 3.4B | Apache 2.0 | 73.6 | Writes sentences not on the page | mlx-vlm |
| Granite-Docling | 0.26B | Open | 4.7 | No text for 56 of 60 lines | MLX |

Sources: [withinnoise.dev](https://withinnoise.dev/blog/posts/ocr-benchmark/); [GLM-OCR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10910v2); [ExpertHTR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12705); [AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/handwriting-recognition); [Chandra](https://github.com/datalab-to/chandra); [PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 card](https://huggingface.co/PaddlePaddle/PaddleOCR-VL-1.6); [ggml-org GLM-OCR-GGUF](https://huggingface.co/ggml-org/GLM-OCR-GGUF/discussions).

**GLM-OCR is the best balance for a Mac.** Its accuracy is within about 1 point of the leaders on IAM lines. On the harder in-house handwriting test from its own report, it scores 87.0, essentially tied with PaddleOCR-VL-1.5 (87.4), far ahead of DeepSeek-OCR 2 (73.8), dots.ocr (71.7) and MinerU 2.5 (54.2), and only 3 points behind Gemini 3 Pro ([GLM-OCR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10910v2)). That report is written by the GLM-OCR team, so treat it with some care. In the same report it is the fastest model: **0.67 images/s, against 0.39 for PaddleOCR-VL-1.5 and 0.10 for dots.ocr** ([GLM-OCR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10910v2)). On an Apple Silicon CPU, with no GPU, it read a cropped handwritten word in **141 ms** ([dev.to](https://dev.to/kaniel_outis/how-well-can-ocr-read-doctor-handwriting-in-2026-54hn)). It also has the easiest Mac setup: official GGUF files from ggml-org, llama.cpp support and Ollama ([GLM-OCR-GGUF](https://huggingface.co/ggml-org/GLM-OCR-GGUF/discussions)).

**PaddleOCR-VL 1.6 is the closest alternative**, especially if you also write Chinese: its handwriting error on Chinese (0.034 edit distance) is much lower than on English (0.118) ([PaddleOCR-VL paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14528)). A 4-bit MLX build is only about 0.68 GB ([mlx-community](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/PaddleOCR-VL-1.6-4bit)). Its results vary a lot between studies, though. It scored 95.8% on IAM lines but **47.48% CER** on the OmniHandwritingOCR English set (an older version, under a strict prompt) ([OmniHandwritingOCR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18586)) and 6.25% page CER in ExpertHTR, against 3.71% for LightOnOCR ([ExpertHTR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12705)). This instability is the main reason GLM-OCR is the first choice.

**Do not use DeepSeek-OCR 2, Granite-Docling, Surya OCR 2 or MinerU 2.5 for handwriting.** DeepSeek-OCR 2 sometimes writes fluent sentences that are not on the page, and Surya OCR 2 invents an extra line after reading the real one ([withinnoise.dev](https://withinnoise.dev/blog/posts/ocr-benchmark/)). **Chandra 2 and olmOCR 2 are accurate but a poor fit for a Mac**: olmOCR needs an NVIDIA GPU, and Chandra's weights have a revenue-limited license ([Chandra](https://github.com/datalab-to/chandra); [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/ocr-open-models)). **CHURRO** is built for historical documents (70.1% similarity on historical handwriting) and does worse than LightOnOCR on modern IAM (7.80% vs 3.71% CER), so it is the wrong tool for modern notes ([CHURRO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768v1); [ExpertHTR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12705)).

### General open VLMs: Qwen leads, Gemma and InternVL lag

Among general-purpose open vision-language models, the **Qwen family is clearly best at handwriting**. On the OmniHandwritingOCR benchmark, which penalizes any "correction" of the writer's words, Qwen3-VL-8B (16.44% CER on English handwriting) matched Qwen2.5-VL-72B (16.01%) and beat InternVL3-78B (20.36%), GPT-4o (20.05%) and Gemma-3-27B (26.12%) ([OmniHandwritingOCR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18586)). On Brazilian-Portuguese handwritten pages, Qwen3-VL-8B scored 9.18% CER, Gemma 4 31B 16.30%, and the small Gemma 3 4B 85.23% ([Escriba-OCR-1B card](https://huggingface.co/tardellirs/Escriba-OCR-1B)). Accuracy falls quickly below about 8B for Qwen3-VL (Qwen3-VL-2B: 38.61%), although the newer Qwen3.5 2B did well on simple IAM lines (97.1%) ([withinnoise.dev](https://withinnoise.dev/blog/posts/ocr-benchmark/)). **InternVL3.5-8B and MiniCPM-V 4.5 were unstable** on Urdu handwriting: one produced fluent but unrelated text and the other added English comments ([Urdu study](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.01635)). In practice, a general VLM is only worth using locally if you want one model for many tasks; for handwriting alone, the smaller OCR models are as accurate and faster.

**MiniCPM-o 4.5** is the one open model built for live video streams. It is 9B, accepts video at up to 10 fps, and runs on an Apple M4 Max with 24 GB or in about 11 GB at int4 ([MiniCPM-o 4.5 card](https://huggingface.co/openbmb/MiniCPM-o-4_5)). No one has measured it on handwriting, and its sibling MiniCPM-V 4.5 was unstable on handwriting, so it is not a good choice for accurate transcription.

## Specialized HTR models are fastest but trained on clean scans

Dedicated handwritten-text-recognition (HTR) models are much smaller than VLMs and still reach the lowest published error rates on the standard **IAM line test**:

| Model | Size | IAM line CER | Training data | Speed evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DRetHTR-Base (Feb 2026) | 107M | **2.26%** | Synthetic + IAM | ~0.04 s per line (batched, A100) |
| DTrOCR | 105M | 2.38% | 2B synthetic lines, 36-character set | – |
| TrOCR-Large / Base / Small | 558M / 334M / smaller | 2.89% / 3.42% / 4.22% | 702M synthetic lines, 36-character set | Large ~0.93 s per line (batched, A100) |
| HTR-ConvText (Dec 2025) | 65.9M | 4.0% | IAM only | CTC (fast) |
| HTR-VT | 53.5M | 4.7% | IAM only | CTC (fast) |
| PyLaia (Transkribus engine) | ~8M | 4.4% (with language model) | IAM | **~10 ms per line** without LM |

Sources: [DRetHTR](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17387); [HTR survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08417); [HTR-ConvText](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.05021v1); [PyLaia](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18722v1). The per-line times are my arithmetic from DRetHTR's full-test-set timings.

These numbers look better than the VLMs', but they are **not directly comparable**. TrOCR and DTrOCR fold case and punctuation into a 36-character set; when TrOCR was re-trained with the full character set, its CER rose to 7.3% ([HTR survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08417)). No study has tested a 2026 VLM and these line models on the same IAM lines with the same scoring ([HTR-ConvText](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.05021v1)). On full pages, zero-shot VLMs already beat general specialized models: on IAM pages, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet had about 1.7% CER against 9.13% for Transkribus Text Titan I, which was not tuned on IAM ([Crosilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15195v2)). **Transkribus wins on historical and non-English archives** (READ2016 German: 40.63% vs 71%+ for LLMs) ([Crosilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15195v2)), which matters little for modern live notes.

The real advantages of specialized models are **speed and safe failure**. CTC models such as PyLaia, HTR-VT and HTR-ConvText read a line in tens of milliseconds ([PyLaia](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18722v1)), and when they fail they produce visibly wrong letters, not fluent invented sentences. TrOCR also runs on phones: on Snapdragon chips its encoder takes about 24–53 ms and each decoding step about 2 ms ([Qualcomm TrOCR](https://huggingface.co/qualcomm/TrOCR)). The weaknesses are that they read one line at a time, need a separate line detector, and are trained on scanned pages, not webcam frames. Use one only if you need under 100 ms per line or want to run on very small hardware. **Avoid Apple's built-in Vision text recognition for handwriting**: developers report weak handwriting accuracy and a regression in newer revisions ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/842742)). Google ML Kit's handwriting API reads pen strokes, not images, so it cannot read paper through a camera ([ML Kit](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/digital-ink-recognition/ios)).

## Fifty to 300 lines of your own handwriting cut errors by a quarter to a half

Fine-tuning on one person's handwriting works with very little data. With classic HTR models, **16 lines from a new writer cut CER by about 25% (relative), and 256 lines by about 50%**, and the models resisted overfitting even with tiny datasets ([Kohút & Hradiš](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.06308)). For TrOCR-style Transformer models the gain is about 10% at 16 lines and about 40% at 256 lines ([Kohút & Hradiš 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.19546)).

For small OCR VLMs, fine-tuning gives even bigger gains, because it mainly stops the model from inventing text. A **1B LightOnOCR model, fully fine-tuned on 647 handwritten pages in 85 minutes on one A100 GPU, went from 8.97% to 2.06% CER**. That beat the zero-shot Qwen3-VL-235B (5.60%) and a specialized HTR model (10.33%) ([Escriba-OCR-1B card](https://huggingface.co/tardellirs/Escriba-OCR-1B)). In ExpertHTR, a fine-tuned 0.8B Qwen3.5 model went from 11.55% to **2.92% CER on IAM pages**, and the part of the error caused by inserted, invented text fell from 7.98 to 0.59 points ([ExpertHTR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12705)). One warning: in a Manchu study, small VLMs fine-tuned only on synthetic handwriting did poorly on real handwriting (3B: 24% word accuracy, 7B: 43%), while an 11B model reached 93% ([Manchu OCR](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06761v1)). **Train on real samples of your own writing**, not only on generated handwriting.

Tools exist for each path. GLM-OCR has an official LLaMA-Factory fine-tuning tutorial, and LightOnOCR-2 provides base checkpoints and a Colab LoRA recipe ([GLM-OCR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10910v2); [LightOn blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/lightonai/lightonocr-2)). Unsloth offers free Colab notebooks for Qwen3-VL-8B ([Unsloth](https://docs.unsloth.ai/models/qwen3-vl-run-and-fine-tune)). A LoRA on a 7–11B model fits on one 48 GB GPU ([Manchu OCR](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06761v1)). No source measured fine-tuning time on Apple Silicon, so plan to fine-tune on a rented or Colab GPU and run the result on your Mac. No study has yet measured per-writer fine-tuning of a VLM directly, so the 100–300-line target is an estimate based on the HTR results and the VLM data sizes above. Capture your training lines **with the same camera and lighting** you will use live, because camera photos differ measurably from clean scans ([writer-adaptive HMER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.32513)).

## Build the live pipeline around stable line crops, not raw video

No benchmark tests handwriting from live video, so the design below is built from indirect evidence. Two facts drive it. Camera images cost much more accuracy than model choice: PaddleOCR-VL dropped from 91.93 to 72.19 and MinerU from 90.67 to 70.91 when pages were photographed instead of scanned ([Docsumo](https://www.docsumo.com/blog/best-ocr-models)). And models tend to "autocorrect": they replace what you wrote with a likely word, and on WildHandBench 75–79% of errors by Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.5 Flash and Claude Opus 4.8 were this type, against 49% for humans ([WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959v1)). Autocorrect errors read fluently, so you will not notice them.

| Stage | What to do | Suggested tool | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Capture | Fix the camera above the paper with even light. Process a region only when the frame is stable or the hand has moved away. | Webcam + simple frame differencing | Photographed pages lose ~20 points; blurry frames with a hand in them waste compute |
| 2. Find lines | Correct perspective, then detect text lines and keep only new or changed ones. | PP-OCRv5/v6 mobile detector (~1 s per full page on an M4, including recognition) | Line crops are faster and more accurate than full frames ([PP-OCRv6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13108)) |
| 3. Read locally | Send each new line crop to the model. Use temperature 0, a short output limit (about 64 tokens) and a prompt that says "transcribe exactly, keep spelling mistakes". | **GLM-OCR** via Ollama or llama.cpp; PaddleOCR-VL 1.6 (MLX) as backup | Near-top accuracy at 0.9B; 141 ms per word crop on a Mac CPU |
| 4. Stabilize | Read the same line in 2–3 frames and commit the text only when the readings agree. | Your own code | Small VLMs sometimes loop or invent lines ([ExpertHTR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12705)) |
| 5. Cloud check (optional) | Send committed lines or the finished page to Gemini. Highlight words where Gemini and the local model disagree. | Gemini 3.x Flash for speed; 3.1 Pro for a final pass | Disagreement flagging caught ~76% of remaining errors while flagging only ~4% of the text |
| 6. Personalize | Save every line you correct. After 100–300 lines, fine-tune and replace the stage-3 model. | GLM-OCR (LLaMA-Factory) or LightOnOCR 1B (Colab) | 25–50% fewer errors from writer data |

The disagreement result comes from the historian's test, where cross-checking Gemini 3 Pro with Claude Opus 4.7 and Gemini 3.5 Flash lowered CER from 1.67% to 1.25% ([Generative History](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/when-models-disagree-transcription)). The same source warns against the obvious alternative: asking one LLM to correct another's transcription "often introduced as many errors as it fixed". In a 2025 test, a Claude correction pass raised IAM CER from 1.75% to 8.55% ([Crosilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15195)). **Flag disagreements; never auto-correct.**

On speed, the evidence is thin. GLM-OCR read a word crop in 141 ms on a Mac CPU ([dev.to](https://dev.to/kaniel_outis/how-well-can-ocr-read-doctor-handwriting-in-2026-54hn)), but one tool reported about 11 s for a full page with a 128-token limit on unstated hardware ([mlx4ocr](https://pypi.org/project/mlx4ocr/)). That gap is why the pipeline reads short line crops, not whole frames; expect well under a second per new line on a recent Mac, but measure it on your machine. If you would rather use the cloud end to end, Google's **Gemini Live API accepts camera frames as JPEG at up to 1 frame per second** and charges $1.00 per million image tokens ([Live API docs](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api); [pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). OpenAI's gpt-realtime also accepts images ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Neither vendor publishes handwriting accuracy for its live models, so sending stable still crops to a normal Gemini Flash call is the safer cloud design.

## Conclusion

The question "which model is most accurate?" matters less than it seems. On clean handwriting lines, the best open models (GLM-OCR, dots.mocr, LightOnOCR, PaddleOCR-VL), the best general open VLMs (Qwen3.5/Qwen3-VL) and the best HTR models all sit around 2–4% CER, and Gemini Pro is only a few points better on messy real-world pages. What separates a good live system from a bad one is capture quality, reading stable line crops, and preventing "autocorrect" errors. Two cheap models that disagree tell you more than one expensive model that sounds confident.

The most useful next step is therefore to collect data, not to read more benchmarks. Write 50 lines in your normal style, capture them with your actual camera, type the correct text, and run GLM-OCR, PaddleOCR-VL 1.6 and one Gemini Flash model on them. That one hour gives you a benchmark for your handwriting and your camera, which no published study covers, and it tests the 2026 models that have no public handwriting results yet. Keep adding the lines you correct during daily use; at a few hundred lines, fine-tuning a 1B model (on a rented GPU) and running it on your Mac becomes the step most likely to close the remaining gap with the cloud models on your own handwriting.
